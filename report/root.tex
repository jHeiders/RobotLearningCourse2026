\begin{abstract}

We report a course project on multi-task reinforcement learning with Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC) on Meta-World, carried out in three stages: single tasks, the three-task suite MT3, and
the ten-task suite MT10. Each stage exposed a different constraint. On single tasks it was
critic stability, fixed by running many environments in parallel and shortening the effective
horizon. On MT3 an apparent allocation problem was in fact a capacity problem: enlarging the
shared learner solved all three tasks under an equal environment split, so we kept no per-task
allocation. On MT10 that configuration solves eight tasks quickly, reaches a ninth late, and
leaves the tenth at zero success throughout. Measuring the reward a random policy can reach
shows a spread of a factor of 85 across the suite, and that the hardest tasks supply too few of
the samples in each training batch for the shared critic to fit them. Weighting replay by each
task's measured failure rate, using no constant that names a task, solves the task that
otherwise never succeeds and raises mean success from $0.877$ to $0.967$ at the same 9M-step
budget.

\end{abstract}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{INTRODUCTION}

A robot that is useful in more than one situation has to hold more than one skill at a time.
Training a separate policy per skill does not scale: each policy needs its own data, its own
tuning and its own storage, and nothing learned in one transfers to the next. Multi-task
reinforcement learning instead asks a single policy to solve a set of tasks at once, so that
shared structure, such as the same arm, the same observation space and the same notion of
reaching and grasping, is learned once rather than repeatedly. Where the tasks really do share
structure, this should cost less experience in total than training them apart, and we
find exactly that on MT3 (Sec.~\ref{sec:mt3vs1}). The resulting policy is also a single model
to deploy and maintain rather than one per skill.

The difficulty is that a shared learner also shares its failure modes. One network, one
replay buffer and one loss now serve tasks that differ in how hard they are to explore, how
much reward they expose early, and how long they take to become learnable at all. Nothing in
the algorithm balances those differences, and an average over tasks can look healthy while
individual tasks receive almost none of the learning signal.

Meta-World \cite{yu2020metaworld}, the standard benchmark for this in robot manipulation,
is designed so that its tasks are genuinely related, which makes it a fair setting in which to
ask whether a shared learner is enough on its own. Our project followed its suites upwards,
from one task to three and then to ten, treating each stage as a test of which of our
assumptions survived the increase in task count.

Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item A staged empirical study of SAC \cite{haarnoja2018sac} on Meta-World across single
      tasks, MT3 and MT10, recording which configuration choices changed the outcome and which
      did not (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}), together with every modification we made to base
      SAC and the stage that motivated it (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A measurement-driven diagnosis of the MT10 failure, ruling out three plausible
      explanations by cheap CPU probes of a saved policy rather than by further training runs
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:mt10stall}).
\item A demonstration that a replay curriculum driven only by self-measured success makes
      a task learnable that a uniform split never learns, at no lasting cost to the tasks
      already solved (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{RELATED WORK}

This section sets out the prior work that informed our design choices and says what we
took from each line of it.

\textbf{Multi-task RL on Meta-World.} Most work on this benchmark targets representation
sharing: soft modularisation routes a shared network per task \cite{yang2020softmod}, and
CARE mixes a set of encoders using task metadata \cite{sodhani2021care}. These change what
the network computes, which is orthogonal to the question we pursue: we keep a single
shared network throughout and change only which data reaches it.

\textbf{Task curricula.} Weighting task sampling by measured failure rate is a standard
remedy for imbalanced multi-task training, typically as $p_i \propto f_i / \sum_j f_j$
applied to episode collection \cite{matiisen2020tscl}. Reweighting what a learner
replays, rather than what it collects, is the idea behind prioritized experience replay
\cite{schaul2016per} and, at the level of whole levels or tasks, prioritized level replay
\cite{jiang2021plr}. Our rule combines the two: failure-rate weights applied to replay, with
a floor and a cap, for the reasons in Sec.~\ref{sec:curriculum}.

\textbf{Critic regularisation.} LayerNorm after each hidden critic layer is common to
several recent value-learning stabilisers \cite{nauman2024bro,lee2025simba,bhatt2024crossq}.
We adopted it from that work for the value-explosion problem described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:multitask}. We did not run an ablation isolating it, so we report it as a
design choice taken from the literature rather than as an effect we measured ourselves.

\subsection{Soft Actor-Critic}

SAC \cite{haarnoja2018sac} is an off-policy actor-critic algorithm for continuous
control. A \emph{critic} $Q(s,a)$ estimates the return obtainable from state $s$ after action
$a$, and an \emph{actor} $\pi(a \mid s)$ proposes actions that score highly under it. Both are
neural networks. Experience is stored in a \emph{replay buffer} and reused many times, which
is what makes the method sample-efficient: each environment step can contribute to many
gradient steps.

Three of its properties matter for what follows. First, the critic is fitted by
minimising the squared difference between its prediction and a bootstrapped target, averaged
over a \emph{batch} of transitions drawn from the buffer. Which transitions are in that batch
therefore decides what the critic learns, and this is the quantity our curriculum controls.
Second, returns are discounted by a factor $\gamma \in (0,1)$, so a per-step reward bounded by
$r_{\max}$ implies a return bounded by $r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$; the discount thus sets the
numerical scale the critic has to represent. Third, SAC adds an entropy bonus to the
objective, which keeps the policy stochastic and therefore exploring; the strength of that
bonus is tuned automatically during training. We refer to the resulting action noise when we
rule out exploration collapse in Sec.~\ref{sec:mt10stall}.

\subsection{Stabilising the critic on single tasks}
\label{sec:critic}

Two settings were forced on us at the single-task stage and kept everywhere afterwards.
Sec.~\ref{sec:single} reports the runs that produced them.

The first is running many environments in parallel rather than one. Consecutive
transitions from a single environment are strongly correlated, so a batch drawn from a buffer
filled by one environment covers a narrow region of the state space. The critic then fits that
region well and extrapolates badly outside it, and because its own targets are bootstrapped
from those extrapolations the error compounds. Running many environments decorrelates the
buffer and removes that feedback loop.

The second is the discount. With a per-step reward bounded by $10$, the bound
$r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$ is $200$ at $\gamma = 0.95$ but $1{,}000$ at $\gamma = 0.99$. The lower
ceiling keeps the values the critic must represent small, which both reduces the size of the
bootstrapped errors above and, once one critic serves many tasks, keeps the tasks' value
magnitudes closer together. The cost is a shorter effective horizon, which we could afford
because Meta-World episodes are capped at 200 steps.

\subsection{Going multi-task}
\label{sec:multitask}

For a suite of $N$ tasks, all tasks run concurrently in a vectorised environment and an
$N$-dimensional one-hot identifying the task is appended to every observation. A single actor
and a single critic serve all tasks, conditioned on which task they are in, and one replay
buffer is shared. This is the standard multi-task SAC (MT-SAC) baseline, and it is what makes
sharing possible: the network can reuse whatever the tasks have in common and use the one-hot
only where they differ.

Sharing does introduce two quantities that no longer take care of themselves. The critic
must now represent value functions of very different magnitudes in one set of weights, and the
buffer must be divided between tasks. The first is why we add LayerNorm after each hidden
critic layer from MT3 onwards, following \cite{nauman2024bro,lee2025simba,bhatt2024crossq}:
normalising activations within each layer keeps them at a consistent scale regardless of the
magnitude of the values being fitted, which is what stops a large-valued task from dominating
the shared representation. We introduced it at MT3 rather than earlier because a single-task
critic fits one scale and never faces the problem. The second quantity, how the buffer is
divided, is the subject of the curriculum learning in Sec.~\ref{sec:curriculum}.

\subsection{Failure-rate replay curriculum}
\label{sec:curriculum}

The last modification is a single rule, and Sec.~\ref{sec:mt10stall} is the measurement that
motivates it. Let $\bar{s}_i$ be task $i$'s mean success over the last $M = 3$ evaluations.
We weight replay sampling by
%
$$
w_i \;\propto\; \mathrm{clip}\left(1 - \bar{s}_i,\; \ell,\; c\right),
\qquad \ell = 0.1,\; c = 0.6
\eqno{(1)}
$$
%
normalised over tasks and applied as a per-environment sampling probability inside the
replay buffer. Task-to-environment assignment is fixed at construction, so this costs one
weighted draw per batch and no per-transition bookkeeping.

Three choices distinguish this from the usual formulation.

\textbf{It acts on replay, not collection.} How often a task is \emph{visited} and how
often it is \emph{trained on} are separate quantities in an off-policy algorithm, because the
buffer sits between them. Collecting more episodes of a failing task does not help if the
batch still draws from it in proportion to a tenth of the buffer, and it costs environment
steps. Reweighting replay changes what the critic actually fits, at no cost in environment
steps, and it can draw on transitions collected long ago. Sec.~\ref{sec:mt10stall} shows that
collection was never the binding constraint on MT10: under a uniform split the suite collects
900k environment steps of each task over the budget, more than twice the 400k that were enough
to solve pick-place on its own, so the failing tasks were not short of experience but short of
attention.

\textbf{The floor $\ell$.} Without it a task at success $1.00$ receives weight zero and
leaves the batch entirely, and the shared weights then drift away from its solution as they
move to accommodate the others. The floor guarantees every solved task a residual share and so
prevents that forgetting.

\textbf{The cap $c$.} Without it, a bare $f_i/\sum_j f_j$ rule gives two tasks stuck at
zero success the whole batch between them. That forgets the solved tasks, and gains the failing
ones nothing, since their difficulty is not a shortage of their own data. The cap bounds the
ratio between the most and least sampled task at $c/\ell = 6$.

We set $\ell$ and $c$ once, from the requirement that a solved task retain a
non-negligible share while no task can monopolise the batch, and did not tune them further; we
report in Sec.~\ref{sec:results} what the resulting shares were and what they cost. On MT10
with eight tasks solved and two failing, eq. (1) gives each failing task about $30\%$ of every
batch and each solved task about $5\%$, against $10\%$ each under a uniform split.

\subsection{No per-task constants}

No configuration file in this project names a task. The curriculum reads evaluation
success, which the run measures, and the only hand-chosen constants, $\ell$, $c$ and the number
of environments, are global. Buffer size, batch size, gradient steps and total steps follow by
multiplying their MT3 values by the ratio of task counts, so each task keeps the replay
capacity and the gradient steps per collected transition it had on MT3. A larger suite would
therefore need no further search.

\section{EXPERIMENTAL SETUP}

We use Meta-World v3 \cite{yu2020metaworld} through Gymnasium, with Stable-Baselines3's SAC
\cite{raffin2021sb3} as the learner. Episodes are capped at 200 steps. Success is
Meta-World's binary per-task criterion, evaluated on 30 episodes per task every 100k
environment steps. Network sizes and all hyperparameters, including the per-stage values
of the settings that scale with task count, are given in Appendix~A.

All runs used a single NVIDIA T1000 (8\,GB) with 20 CPU cores at roughly 53 environment
steps per second, so one 9M-step MT10 run takes about 47 hours; this is why MT10 is compared on
a single seed and two configurations.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}
\label{sec:experiments}

MT10 is the setting this report focuses on. The single-task and MT3 stages are reported
because they are where the configuration MT10 inherits was decided, and because the MT3
result is what led us away from per-task allocation before we ever reached MT10.
Neither stage is a controlled ablation: several settings change between rows, and we
report them as development steps.

\subsection{Single tasks}
\label{sec:single}

Training SAC on reach, push and pick-place individually gave us the
reference points in Table~\ref{tab:single} and the two settings of Sec.~\ref{sec:critic}.

Our initial push run used a single environment and $\gamma = 0.99$. It reached $0.20$
success early, then collapsed to $0.00$ and stayed there, reproducibly, with critic values
growing without bound. Both mechanisms of Sec.~\ref{sec:critic} were acting at once. The
single environment made consecutive samples in each batch highly correlated, so the critic was
fitted on a narrow slice of the state space and its bootstrapped targets were drawn from
regions it had never seen; and $\gamma = 0.99$ allowed a value five times larger before any
divergence became visible, so the errors had room to grow. Because the actor follows the
critic, a critic that has diverged points the policy at states that do not deliver the value
it predicts, which is why success collapses rather than merely stalling. Moving to 16
environments and $\gamma = 0.95$ took the same task to $1.00$. We changed both at once and did
not separate them afterwards, so we cannot attribute the recovery to one alone; we report the
combination as the configuration that worked and kept it for every later run.

\subsection{MT3: capacity, not allocation}
\label{sec:mt3}

MT3, comprising reach, push and pick-place, is where we move to multi-task training,
where the corresponding failure modes first appear, and where runs are cheap enough to
iterate. Pick-place is the only one of the three ever in doubt, and the three runs below are
quoted at each run's final evaluation, on the full 6.0M-step budget except where noted.
Our first run, on an equal $5/5/5$ environment split, left pick-place at $0.00$ success
while the other two reached $1.00$. Hand-allocating environments towards it, at $2/4/9$,
took it to $0.93$, and that is what first suggested an allocation problem.

What changed our mind is that restoring the equal split, at $9/9/9$, while enlarging
the shared learner takes pick-place to $1.00$ with all three tasks solved, at which point
early stopping fires at 5.0M steps. Nothing in that configuration is directed towards
pick-place in particular. Three of those changes act on the difficulty identified in
Sec.~\ref{sec:multitask}. Raising the number of parallel environments from 5 to 9 per task and
the buffer from 300k to 1M transitions gives each task more decorrelated data of its own;
raising the batch from 128 to 384 means each gradient step still sees a usable number of
samples per task once the batch is divided three ways; and raising the gradient steps per
collection round from 10 to 18 buys more optimisation from the same environment steps, which
is what a task learned late needs most. Adding LayerNorm to the critic addresses the value
scale directly. We changed these together, so the outcome belongs to the combination
rather than to any single one. Per-run settings are in Appendix~A.

What the comparison establishes is that the hand-tuned allocation was not necessary: a
configuration that never refers to a particular task reaches at least as far. That was enough
to drop per-task allocation before moving to MT10, where at 47 hours per run only a single
controlled comparison is affordable.

\subsection{MT3 against single-task}
\label{sec:mt3vs1}

The scaled MT3 run reaches $1.00$ success on all three of reach, push and pick-place. The
single-task runs of Table~\ref{tab:single} reach $1.00$, $0.93$ and $0.80$ success on the
same tasks, using 2.0M environment steps each, or 6.0M in total. MT3 matches or
exceeds all three while using
5.0M environment steps in total across the suite, and it does so with one network instead of
three. This is the concrete form of the claim made in the introduction: on tasks that share
structure, learning them together cost us less experience than learning them apart, not more.
We make no claim about which shared component is responsible.

\subsection{MT10: the stall, and what it is not}
\label{sec:mt10stall}

We carried the MT3 configuration to MT10 unchanged except for the quantities that are
functions of task count (Appendix~A). It solved eight of ten tasks by 1.5M steps
and then held mean success flat at $0.79$ for the next one million steps, with both remaining
tasks at $0.00$ success. Scaling capacity was therefore not by itself the answer either.

Three explanations were consistent with what we could see, and we tested all three on CPU
by probing a saved policy rather than by launching further training runs. None holds.
\emph{Exploration collapse}: SAC's entropy bonus is tuned automatically, and had it decayed on
the failing tasks the policy would have become too deterministic to stumble into a sparse
success condition. It is contradicted as strongly as possible, since those two tasks carry the
largest action noise in the run, not the smallest, so the standard remedy of a per-task entropy
coefficient would push exploration where it is already highest. \emph{Insufficient retention}:
the buffer is shared, so a task holds only a fraction of it and could be fitting scarce data.
But that fraction is the same number of transitions that solved pick-place on its own at the
single-task stage. \emph{Episode cap}: our 200-step limit is shorter than Meta-World's default,
so a task needing longer would register zero success however well it was learning. It does bound
one task, since Meta-World's scripted expert exceeds 200 steps on a minority of attempts, but
the resulting ceiling near $85\%$ cannot explain a floor of exactly zero. The measurements are
in Appendix~C.

A fourth explanation is the one that survived, and it concerns how much reward a task
exposes before it has been learned. Rolling out a random policy on each task and recording
the largest per-step reward it encounters within an episode, averaged over episodes and
seeds, gives Table~\ref{tab:reward}. The spread across the suite is a factor of 85.

The ordering reproduces the order in which the ten tasks are learned. The two tasks at
the bottom are exactly the two that are slow or never learned, and push, the least stable of
the eight solved tasks, is next-lowest. Its consequence for a
shared critic is arithmetic. Once the easy tasks are solved their evaluation returns are
more than fifteen times larger than an unlearned peg-insert-side episode's (Appendix~C), and
the critic is fitted by minimising squared error over a batch containing all tasks, so
residuals enter the gradient in proportion to the square of their magnitude, a gap of more
than two orders of magnitude. The signal belonging to the
failing task is not merely smaller than the others, it is negligible against them, and the
critic reduces its loss most efficiently by ignoring the task.

Two levers follow, and they are not equivalent. One is the weight each residual carries in
the loss; the other is how many samples in the batch belong to the task at all, which under
a uniform split over ten tasks is a tenth of them. We act on the second, because no
reweighting of a loss creates data that is not in the batch. That is the curriculum of
Sec.~\ref{sec:curriculum}, and Sec.~\ref{sec:curriculum} explains there why the weights
are applied to replay rather than to collection: on this suite the failing tasks were already
being visited far more than enough.

\subsection{MT10: curriculum against baseline}
\label{sec:results}

The baseline here is the identical configuration with the curriculum disabled, so that
replay is drawn uniformly across the ten tasks. Both runs go to 9M environment steps and
neither uses early stopping, so a task at zero is seen to stay at zero for the whole budget
rather than being cut short. Fig.~\ref{fig:mean} gives mean success and
Fig.~\ref{fig:hard} the two slow tasks; the eight remaining tasks are solved by both runs, and
Appendix~B gives the full per-task breakdown.

\textbf{Baseline.} Eight tasks pass $0.9$ success by 1.5M steps and mean success then holds at
$0.79$ for the next one million steps. That plateau is what prompted the diagnosis above, and
it does not last: pick-place records its first non-zero evaluation at 2.5M steps,
passes half success at 5.0M steps, and ends at $0.83$ success. Peg-insert-side does not move at
all, reading exactly $0.00$ success at every one of its 91 evaluations, with its return at the
reward floor throughout. Mean success over the suite ends at $0.877$. The two failing
tasks are therefore not alike: one is slow and one does not learn.

\textbf{Curriculum.} Peg-insert-side leaves zero success at 2.1M steps and reaches
$1.00$ success,
meaning all 30 evaluation episodes succeed, 300k steps later, having sat at exactly zero
throughout the preceding 2.1M steps. It ends at $0.90$ success, against $0.00$ for the
baseline. Pick-place leaves zero success at 0.8M steps and
passes half success at 1.9M steps, against 5.0M steps for the baseline, and ends at $0.80$
success against the baseline's $0.83$. Mean
success ends at $0.967$, and is stable there: over the final million steps it stays
between $0.967$ and $1.000$.

The two comparisons are not of the same kind. The one that carries the result is
peg-insert-side: 91 consecutive zeros against a task solved outright. The other is
pick-place, where both runs arrive but the curriculum arrives $2.6\times$ sooner and
at the same place. The curriculum enables
one task and accelerates another; it does nothing for the eight that were never in doubt.
Its scope is that specific: it is a remedy for starved tasks, not a general improvement
to MT-SAC.

\textbf{The mechanism is visible directly.} Fig.~\ref{fig:share} plots the share of each
batch the curriculum assigns to each task. Both slow tasks rise from the uniform tenth to
roughly $30\%$ within the first million steps and hold it while they are failing. The moment a
task is solved its share collapses back to the floor, which is what eq. (1) prescribes and what
makes the rule self-limiting: peg-insert-side's share falls at 2.5M steps, immediately after
it reaches $1.00$ success at 2.4M. Pick-place oscillates afterwards because its success oscillates,
and the weights track it within three evaluations. This is also what the diagnosis of
Sec.~\ref{sec:mt10stall} could not supply. Reachable reward orders the tasks correctly, but it
leaves pick-place and peg-insert-side a factor under three apart while one is learned and the
other is not; what separated them was the number of transitions each contributed to a batch,
and once peg-insert-side's tripled it became learnable within 300k steps.

\textbf{What it costs.} Diverting replay towards failing tasks slows the ones already
working: the baseline has eight tasks above $0.9$ success at 1.5M steps, the curriculum not
until 2.0M. The cost does not persist. At the endpoint the curriculum is at or above the
baseline on all eight, and push, the least stable of them in both runs, is slightly steadier
under the curriculum.

\textbf{Against single-task SAC.} At each run's final evaluation, the curriculum run
reaches $1.00$ success on reach against $1.00$ in Table~\ref{tab:single}, $0.97$ on push
against $0.93$, and $0.80$ on pick-place against $0.80$, and it also solves peg-insert-side,
which we never trained standalone. It therefore matches or exceeds single-task SAC on every
task we trained separately, despite dividing its capacity ten ways and collecting under half as
many environment steps per task, since the 9M budget is a total across the suite. We make no
claim of positive transfer, only that the usual multi-task penalty did not appear.

\section{LIMITATIONS}

\textbf{Single seed.} Every number comes from one seed per configuration; at 47 hours per
MT10 run, seed replication was not affordable alongside the comparison. A task at exactly
$0.00$ success across 91 consecutive evaluations against $1.00$ success on 30 episodes is not
seed noise, but the mean-success difference should be read as indicative rather than
significant, and the pick-place endpoints are well inside seed noise.

\textbf{The mechanism is not isolated.} We did not vary the batch share independently of
the curriculum, so our reading that the share is what made peg-insert-side learnable is the
most plausible account of our data rather than a controlled finding.

\textbf{The episode cap.} Meta-World's scripted expert policies, which serve as a
reference for how many steps a task needs, exceed 200 steps on a minority of peg-insert-side
attempts, implying a ceiling near $85\%$ at our cap. The learned policy nonetheless reaches
$1.00$ success, so it completes the task in fewer steps than the scripted controller and the
cap is not binding here.

\textbf{Not tested.} A per-task entropy coefficient is implemented and left disabled, on
the measurement that the failing tasks were already the noisiest in the run; testing it would
strengthen the comparison against the usual remedy. We also did not attempt MT30 or MT50, so
the claim that the configuration transfers by arithmetic is untested.
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{CONCLUSIONS}

We scaled SAC from single Meta-World tasks to MT10, and each stage produced an insight
the previous one could not. On single tasks the constraint was critic stability, resolved by
decorrelating the replay buffer and bounding the value scale. On MT3 an apparent allocation
problem turned out to be a capacity problem, and enlarging the shared learner solved all three
tasks without directing resources at any of them; learning the three together also cost less
total experience than learning them apart. On MT10 a third constraint appeared that neither
earlier account covers, in which a task exposing very little reward before it is learned
receives too small a share of each batch for the shared critic to fit it. A replay curriculum
weighted by measured failure rate addresses exactly that, solving the task that otherwise never
succeeds, reaching the slow task $2.6\times$ earlier, and improving mean success from $0.877$
to $0.967$ at the final evaluation.

The general lesson is about where to look when a shared learner stalls. The interaction
that a shared critic and a shared buffer create between tasks is invisible in an average over
tasks, and diagnosing it required measuring a per-task quantity the algorithm depends on but
does not report. We would repeat that order of operations: measure what the algorithm consumes
per task before changing what it does, and keep every setting free of any reference to a
particular task.

\textbf{Exploration.} Per-task action standard deviation: peg-insert-side is the
noisiest task in the run at $0.877$ and pick-place second at $0.531$, against $0.272$

under a uniform split, matching the retention that solved pick-place on MT3. At a 500-step

$1{,}081$ to $1{,}867$, while an unlearned peg-insert-side episode returns under $70$.

which is a max statistic: it grows with the episode count and varied by more than a factor of
two between seeds, while the reported quantity is stable to the second decimal.

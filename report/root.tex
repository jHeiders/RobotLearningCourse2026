\begin{abstract}

This report documents our course project on multi-task reinforcement learning with Soft
Actor-Critic (SAC) on the Meta-World benchmark, from single tasks through MT3 to MT10.
Our baseline multi-task agent learns eight of the ten MT10 tasks and leaves
\emph{pick-place} and \emph{peg-insert-side} at exactly zero success for as long as we
train it. Rather than tuning further, we tried to establish why. Measuring the peak
per-step reward a random policy encounters gives $3.59$ on \emph{reach} but $0.03$ on
\emph{peg-insert-side} --- a 120-fold spread that matches the order in which the ten tasks
are learned. Since the solved tasks reach returns near $1{,}800$, the residuals belonging
to the failing tasks are negligible in the shared squared-error critic loss. Acting on
this, we normalise the critic loss by a per-task running estimate of return scale, an
adaptation of PopArt, and combine it with a replay curriculum weighted by measured failure
rate. Both mechanisms read only statistics the run computes about itself, so no per-task
constants are involved. Mean success on MT10 rises from $0.79$ to $0.973$ and
\emph{peg-insert-side} reaches $1.00$, having never succeeded once under the baseline or
under the curriculum alone. We report an ablation over the two mechanisms, a comparison
against single-task SAC, and the limitations of a single-seed study.

\end{abstract}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{INTRODUCTION}

Multi-task reinforcement learning asks a single policy to solve several tasks at once.
Meta-World \cite{yu2020metaworld} is the standard benchmark for this in robot manipulation:
a Sawyer arm, a shared observation and action space, and task suites of increasing size.
The natural baseline, and the one this project builds on, is Soft Actor-Critic
\cite{haarnoja2018sac} trained on all tasks simultaneously with a task one-hot appended to
the observation and one shared replay buffer --- multi-task SAC (MT-SAC).

MT-SAC has a characteristic failure mode. On MT10 it learns most tasks quickly and
completely, then stalls: eight tasks reach $1.00$ within roughly 1.5M environment steps,
and \emph{pick-place} and \emph{peg-insert-side} remain at exactly $0.00$ for as long as
training continues. They do not learn slowly; they do not learn at all. Their evaluation
return sits at the reward floor from the first evaluation onwards.

The intuitive reading is a data-allocation problem --- the hard tasks are a tenth of the
experience and need a larger share --- and the common remedy is a curriculum that samples
failing tasks more often. We implemented that remedy, and it was not sufficient. The rest
of this report is mostly an account of finding out why.

We set ourselves the constraint that no setting may name an individual task. Hand-allocating
resources to whichever task is currently failing does work, but it amounts to tuning the
configuration until the benchmark passes, and it does not carry over to a larger task set.
We therefore only accepted mechanisms that adapt from quantities the run measures about
itself while it runs.

Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnosis} reports the diagnosis: what we ruled out, and the measurement that
identified reward scale as the binding constraint. Sec.~\ref{sec:method} describes the two
mechanisms we added --- a replay curriculum and a per-task normalisation of the critic loss,
the latter an adaptation of PopArt \cite{vanhasselt2016popart,hessel2019popart} to a shared
continuous-control critic. Sec.~\ref{sec:results} gives the MT10 ablation, a comparison
against single-task SAC, and what the experiments cost us in solved-task stability.

\section{BACKGROUND}

Everything we use is established; this section records where it comes from.

\textbf{Multi-task RL on Meta-World.} Most work on this benchmark targets representation
sharing: soft modularisation routes a shared network per task \cite{yang2020softmod}, and
CARE mixes a set of encoders using task metadata \cite{sodhani2021care}. These change what
the network computes, which is a different question from the one we ended up looking at.

\textbf{Value normalisation.} PopArt \cite{vanhasselt2016popart} normalises value targets
adaptively while preserving the network's outputs, and was applied to multi-task RL
precisely because tasks differ in reward scale \cite{hessel2019popart}. That work uses
discrete actions and separate value heads per task. Our setting has one shared
continuous-control critic whose output SAC's actor consumes directly, so we scale the loss
rather than the targets; this keeps $Q$ in real units and avoids PopArt's
output-preservation correction, at the cost of not normalising the targets themselves.

\textbf{Task curricula.} Weighting task sampling by measured failure rate is a standard
remedy for imbalanced multi-task training, typically as
$p_i \propto f_i / \sum_j f_j$ applied to episode collection. We apply the weights to replay
sampling instead, and add a floor and a cap, for the reasons in Sec.~\ref{sec:curriculum}.

\textbf{Critic regularisation.} LayerNorm after each hidden critic layer is common to
several recent value-learning stabilisers \cite{nauman2024bro,lee2025simba,bhatt2024crossq}.
We use it throughout. It is not something we contribute, but it is what let one
configuration carry from MT3 to MT10 without retuning.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{PRELIMINARIES AND BASELINE}

We use Meta-World v3 \cite{yu2020metaworld} through Gymnasium, with Stable-Baselines3's SAC
\cite{raffin2021sb3} as the learner. All $N$ tasks run concurrently in a vectorised
environment; a $N$-dimensional one-hot identifying the task is appended to each
observation; and one replay buffer is shared by all of them. Episodes are capped at 200
steps. Success is Meta-World's binary per-task criterion, evaluated on 30 episodes per task
every 100k environment steps.

This is MT-SAC with a shared backbone, and it requires no algorithmic change --- the
one-hot is sufficient for the policy and critic to condition on task identity. Every method
in this report is an addition to this baseline, switchable independently.

Two settings deserve comment because they shaped everything downstream. We use
$\gamma = 0.95$ rather than the more common $0.99$: with a per-step reward bounded by 10, the
value of a solved task is bounded by $10/(1-\gamma)$, which is $200$ at $0.95$ but $1{,}000$
at $0.99$, and the larger ceiling made the critic unstable in early runs. And we use many
parallel environments per task at a fixed update-to-data ratio of $0.67$; a single
environment per task diverged reproducibly (Table~\ref{tab:single}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{DIAGNOSIS}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

\subsection{MT3: capacity, not allocation}

We first studied the failure on MT3 (\emph{reach}, \emph{push}, \emph{pick-place}), where
runs are cheap. Table~\ref{tab:mt3} gives three configurations. The reference
hyperparameters leave \emph{pick-place} at $0.00$ after 6M steps. Hand-allocating
environments towards it ($2/4/9$ instead of $5/5/5$) fixes it, reaching $0.93$ --- which is
what suggests an allocation problem in the first place.

The third row is the one that matters. Restoring a \emph{uniform} split while scaling the
learner's capacity --- more parallel environments, a larger replay buffer, a larger batch,
and a proportionally larger number of gradient steps --- solves all three tasks completely.
The hand-tuned allocation was not merely unnecessary; it was slightly worse than not
tuning at all.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{MT3 after 6M environment steps. Final evaluation, 30 episodes per task.}
\label{tab:mt3}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
configuration & envs & buffer & batch & pick-place & mean \\
\midrule
reference hparams   & 5/5/5 & 300k & 128 & 0.00 & 0.60 \\
hand allocation     & 2/4/9 & 300k & 256 & 0.93 & 0.967 \\
uniform, scaled     & 9/9/9 & 1M   & 384 & \textbf{1.00} & \textbf{1.00} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{table}

We carried the third configuration to MT10 unchanged except for the four quantities that
are functions of task count. It solved eight of ten tasks and reproduced the stall exactly.
Allocation was therefore not the MT3 bottleneck, and the MT10 stall needed a different
explanation.

\subsection{What we ruled out}

Three explanations are available and all three are wrong, which we established by probing
the saved MT10 policy on CPU rather than by launching further training runs.

\emph{Exploration collapse.} If the failing tasks had lost their exploration noise they
could not discover the sparse success condition. Measured per-task action standard
deviation contradicts this in the strongest possible way: \emph{peg-insert-side} is the
\emph{noisiest} task in the run at $0.877$, and \emph{pick-place} second at $0.531$,
against $0.272$ for the fully solved \emph{reach}. The failing tasks are not short of
exploration. This also predicts that a per-task entropy coefficient --- the standard
remedy, and the one prior course work applies --- would push where the agent is already
saturated, and we accordingly did not adopt it.

\emph{Insufficient retention.} The shared buffer holds 3.3M transitions across ten tasks,
i.e.\ 330k per task under a uniform split, matching the retention that solved
\emph{pick-place} on MT3. Retention was already correct.

\emph{The episode cap.} Using Meta-World's scripted expert policies we measured
steps-to-success at a 500-step horizon: \emph{peg-insert-side} is solved in a median of 93
steps, and 17 of 20 attempts finish within our 200-step cap. The cap imposes a ceiling of
roughly 85\% on that task, but cannot explain a floor of zero.

\subsection{What the measurement showed}

The decisive quantity is how much reward is reachable \emph{before} a task is learned. We
rolled out a random policy on each task and recorded the peak per-step reward it
encountered:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & reach & push & pick-place & peg-insert-side \\
\midrule
peak reward & 3.59 & 0.54 & 0.08 & 0.03 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This is a 120-fold spread, and it reproduces the exact order in which the ten tasks were
learned. Its consequence for a shared critic is arithmetic. Once the easy tasks are solved
their evaluation returns range from $1{,}081$ to $1{,}867$, while an unlearned
\emph{peg-insert-side} episode returns under $70$. The critic is fitted by minimising
squared error across a batch containing all tasks, so residuals enter the gradient in
proportion to the square of their magnitude: a two-order-of-magnitude gap in return scale
is a four-order-of-magnitude gap in gradient contribution. The signal belonging to
\emph{peg-insert-side} is not merely smaller than the others; it is negligible against
them. The critic reduces its loss most efficiently by ignoring the task, and the actor,
which follows $Q$, ignores it in turn.

This reframes the problem. A curriculum controls \emph{how often} a task is sampled. It
cannot make what that task shows the critic register. The two are orthogonal, and for
\emph{peg-insert-side} the second is the binding constraint.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{METHOD}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Per-task critic-loss normalisation}

We maintain, for each task $i$, an exponential moving estimate $\sigma_i$ of the standard
deviation of that task's TD targets, updated from each batch with momentum
$\alpha = 10^{-3}$:
%
$$
\sigma_i \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,\sigma_i + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{std}\!\left(\{y_k : \tau_k = i\}\right)
\eqno{(1)}
$$
%
where $y_k$ is the target for sample $k$ and $\tau_k$ its task, recovered from the one-hot
block of the observation. The critic loss divides each sample's residual by its own task's
scale:
%
$$
\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\; \mathbb{E}_k
\left[ \left( \frac{Q_j(s_k,a_k) - y_k}{\max(\sigma_{\tau_k},\,\sigma_{\min})} \right)^{\!2} \right]
\eqno{(2)}
$$
%
with $\sigma_{\min} = 10^{-2}$ guarding against a task whose targets are momentarily
constant. Every task now contributes comparably to the loss regardless of its reward
magnitude.

Two properties matter. First, we scale the \emph{loss}, not the targets: $Q$ stays in real
units, so the actor objective, the target computation and every logged value are unchanged,
and none of PopArt's output-preservation correction is required. Second, $\sigma_i$ is
estimated from the run's own TD targets. It is not a constant, not a per-task setting, and
not read from a previous run.

\subsection{Failure-rate replay curriculum}
\label{sec:curriculum}

Let $\bar{s}_i$ be task $i$'s mean success over the last $M = 3$ evaluations. We weight
replay sampling by
%
$$
w_i \;\propto\; \mathrm{clip}\left(1 - \bar{s}_i,\; \ell,\; c\right),
\qquad \ell = 0.1,\; c = 0.6
\eqno{(3)}
$$
%
normalised over tasks and applied as a per-environment sampling probability inside the
replay buffer. Task-to-environment assignment is fixed at construction, so this costs one
weighted draw per batch and no per-transition bookkeeping.

Three choices distinguish this from the usual formulation. It acts on \textbf{replay rather
than collection}, because collection share and optimisation share are separable and only
the latter binds: under a uniform split MT10 already collects far more
\emph{pick-place} transitions than the amount that solved it standalone. The \textbf{floor}
$\ell$ prevents a solved task's share from going to zero, which is forgetting; the
\textbf{cap} $c$ prevents a task that cannot yet be learned from absorbing the entire
budget, which is the failure mode that a bare $f_i / \sum_j f_j$ rule exhibits at ten tasks
where two are stuck at zero. With eight tasks solved and two at zero, (3) gives each failing
task 115 of every 384 samples and each solved task 19.

\subsection{Absence of per-task constants}

No file in this work names a task. The curriculum reads evaluation success, which the run
measures; the normalisation reads TD-target dispersion, which the run computes. The only
hand-chosen constants --- $\ell$, $c$, $\alpha$, $\sigma_{\min}$, and the number of
environments --- are global and shared by every task. Four further settings (buffer size,
batch size, gradient steps, total steps) are functions of the number of tasks and follow by
arithmetic. Applying the same configuration to a larger suite such as MT30 would therefore
need no further search, though we had neither the compute nor the time to verify that, and
MT50 was out of reach on our hardware.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{EXPERIMENTAL SETUP}

All runs use Meta-World 3.1.1, Gymnasium 1.3.0, Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0 and PyTorch 2.13 on
a single NVIDIA T1000 (8\,GB) with 20 CPU cores. Networks are $[512, 1024, 1024, 512]$ for
both actor and critic, with LayerNorm after each hidden critic layer. Learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, $\tau = 10^{-3}$, $\gamma = 0.95$, automatic entropy tuning,
$\log\sigma_{\text{init}} = -3$.

MT10 uses 9 environments per task (90 total), a 3.3M-transition buffer, batch 384, and 60
gradient steps per collection round, which is an update-to-data ratio of $0.67$ --- the same
ratio used on MT3. Evaluation is 30 episodes per task every 100k steps. All three MT10 arms
share this configuration exactly; they differ only in which of the two mechanisms is
enabled.

\section{RESULTS}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{MT10 ablation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} and Table~\ref{tab:mt10} give the three arms. The uniform baseline
plateaus at $0.79$ from 1.4M steps onward with both hard tasks at exactly $0.00$. Adding
the curriculum raises \emph{pick-place} above zero for the first time --- $0.03$, $0.07$,
$0.17$ across successive evaluations --- but it never consolidates, and
\emph{peg-insert-side} does not move at all. This is what convinced us the allocation
reading was wrong: the curriculum verifiably tripled the failing tasks' share of every
gradient step, and it was still not enough.

Adding normalisation changes the outcome. \emph{Peg-insert-side} records its first non-zero
evaluation at 2.7M steps, passes $0.5$ at 3.3M, and reaches $1.00$ --- all 30 evaluation
episodes --- at 5.6M. \emph{Pick-place} follows the same trajectory roughly 800k steps
behind. At 5.9M steps the run reaches a mean success of $0.973$ with nine tasks at or above
$0.9$, and it is the first run in this project in which all ten tasks produce successful
evaluation episodes.

matched step at which the uniform baseline had already plateaued.}

Two observations qualify the result. First, normalisation gives no early advantage: at 2.2M
the normalised arm is marginally \emph{behind} the baseline ($0.777$ against $0.793$). The
mechanism does not accelerate tasks that are already learnable; it makes tasks learnable
that otherwise never become so, and that shows up only after 2.5M steps.

Second, there is a cost. \emph{Push} --- solved and stable at $1.00$ in the baseline ---
becomes noisy under the curriculum, oscillating between $0.80$ and $1.00$, because the floor
$\ell = 0.1$ deliberately reduces a solved task's replay share to 19 samples per batch. The
mean is nonetheless far higher, so the trade is favourable, but it identifies $\ell$ as the
parameter to raise if a solved task were to degrade further.

\subsection{The mechanism is visible in the statistics}

Fig.~\ref{fig:scale} plots the learned per-task value scales $\sigma_i$. They span
$15$ to $89$ at convergence --- a factor of six between the extremes among solved tasks, and
far more early in training. \emph{Peg-insert-side} begins near $0.04$ and rises to $6.7$ as
the task starts to be learned, then to the range of the others. This is a direct measurement
of the imbalance the method corrects: without (2), a residual on the $\sigma \approx 0.04$
task is weighted identically to one on the $\sigma \approx 89$ task, and contributes
$(89/0.04)^2 \approx 5\times10^{6}$ times less to the gradient.

\subsection{Transfer relative to single-task training}

Table~\ref{tab:single} compares the multi-task result against SAC trained on each task
alone for 2M steps; both columns report the final evaluation. The multi-task agent is
within noise of the single-task baselines on every task we measured separately, despite
dividing its capacity ten ways --- and solves \emph{peg-insert-side}, which we never
trained standalone, at $1.00$. Sharing a critic across ten tasks therefore costs nothing
measurable here once the scale imbalance is corrected.

The failed row is included because it motivates the configuration: with one environment per
task and $\gamma = 0.99$, \emph{push} collapses to $0.00$ and stays there. The number of
parallel environments and the discount are not incidental choices.

\section{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}

\textbf{Single seed.} Every number reported here comes from one seed per configuration.
The compute available (one T1000; an MT10 run takes roughly 32 hours for 6M steps) did not
permit seed replication alongside the ablation. The central claim is robust to this in one
respect --- a task moving from exactly $0.00$ across 23 consecutive evaluations to $1.00$ on
30 episodes is not seed noise --- but the mean-success differences between arms should be
read as indicative rather than significant.

\textbf{Unequal arm lengths.} The curriculum-only arm was warm-started from the uniform
arm's checkpoint and run for 1.2M further steps rather than trained from scratch, which
makes it a probe rather than a matched arm. It is reported as such. The uniform and
normalised arms are both from scratch and are directly comparable.

\textbf{The episode cap bounds \emph{peg-insert-side}.} Our scripted-expert measurement
implies a ceiling near 85\% at 200 steps, yet the learned policy reaches $1.00$. The learned
solution is therefore faster than Meta-World's reference controller on this task, which we
did not anticipate and did not optimise for.

\textbf{What we did not test.} A per-task entropy coefficient was implemented and left
disabled, on the measurement that the failing tasks were already the noisiest in the run.
Testing it as a fourth arm would strengthen the comparison against prior work. We also did
not isolate normalisation \emph{without} the curriculum on MT10; the diagnosis predicts it
would recover most of the gain, and that is the single most informative missing experiment.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{CONCLUSIONS}

We set out to scale SAC from single Meta-World tasks to MT10, and spent most of the project
on the two tasks that would not learn. The useful lesson for us was that the obvious
explanation --- too little data --- was testable and false, and that a cheap measurement on
CPU (the reward a random policy can reach on each task) identified the actual constraint
after two full training runs had failed to fix it. Correcting the critic loss for per-task
return scale, together with a replay curriculum, took MT10 mean success from $0.79$ to
$0.973$ and solved \emph{peg-insert-side}, which had never once succeeded beforehand.

Neither mechanism is new in itself; the normalisation is an adaptation of PopArt and the
curriculum is a standard failure-rate rule with a floor and a cap. What we would keep from
this project is the working habit: when a run stalls, measure the quantity the algorithm
actually depends on before changing hyperparameters. The main caveat is that all of our
numbers come from a single seed, and the missing experiment we would run next is
normalisation without the curriculum, to see how much of the gain the curriculum
contributes at all.
